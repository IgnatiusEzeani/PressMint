PARISH CHURCH ORDINA-

TION.

Bishop On Present Momentous

Times.

The Rev. Thos. Mackinson Armour, Who has been acting as lay worker at the Parish Church since Christmas, was ordained deacon by the Bishop of Liverpool at last Sunday morning's service. The service was an impressive one, and the clergy present beside the Bishop, were.: The Archdeacon of Warrington. (the Rev. Canon Howson), the Rector (the Rev. Canon Willis), the Rev. Canon Stevenson, Rev. T. Vickers, Rev. J. M. Buckmaster, and the Rev. S. G. Stanton.

The new curate, who for two years was a student at St. Chad Hall, Durham, recently took his B.A. degree at Durham University. He will work as a deacon for about twelve months, at the end of which time he will be ordained priest. He will have charge of the Howley dis- trict of St. Elphin's parish.

BISHOP CHAVASSE AND SELF-SACRIFICE. His Lordship, preaching upon the "Man sent from God whose name was John," said he had come tit ordain the fourth of their asSistant clergy. He took it that they had discovered the man's worth during the months he had worked amongst them as a layman, and that the large congregation was, in a measure, due to that fact. They had realised his worth and had come to help him on that momentous day in his career by their sympathy and presence. It was, he thought, a useful custom to hold ordinations at other places than the Cathedral, so that the people generally might learn to realise the importance and dignity of the ministry by witnessing an ordination for them- selves and so learn to pray more earnestly for their ministers and to be more ready to dedi- cate their own sons to tie service of God. "It is," proceeded the Bishop, " in a dark and anxious time that, this ordination takes place and unless I am mistaken, as you look back upon it through the mists of years you will always connect it with the appalling war in which we are at present engaged; and not only with this appalling war, but with that moment in it when for the first time it seems as If God were about to bless, and to bless with victory, the arms of the Allies." The Allies Ready.

Unprepared, we had had to face the greatest military power in the world that had been silently and thoroughly preparing for over forty years. It had taken the Allies nearly two years before they could put forth their strength. We had to thank God for the recent naval viet'bry which had, for a time at all events, shattered ani crippled the enemy fleet and so preserved our shores.--We had to thank God for the wonder- ful recovery of Russia who, after the loss of millions of men—he spoke what he knew—had driven the Austrians back in a way that would rank as one of the most remarkable incidents of the war. We had to thank God that Italy had recovered and was now sweeping triumph- antly the army of the invader from the Venetian plain to the mountains. "And," he continued, "for the news that reached us last night we have further to thank God—that- French and English soldiers fighting side by side have partly broken through the lines of the enemy over a space of sixteen to twenty miles, and that when the last account was received last night the report was 'Every- thing going well for the Allies. It is at this moment, when many of us who have sons ; the fighting line are anxious at heart for their safety and when the whole nation is watching with straining eyes this titanic contest that your new curate has been ordained a minister of God and that fact will, I am sure, add verSr iquch to the solemnity of the occasion and add further seriousness to our prayers."

Reality, Self-effacement, Self-sacrifice.

The one thing he wished to fix their thoughts uplan was that their new curate was, like St. John, a man sent from God. Our Church held up St. John the Baptist as a model minister. His characterisiics were reality, self-effacement and self-sacrifice. He was absolutely real, speaking with the strength of conviction: men believed him because he said what he believed and he believed what he said. He effaced. self —he was only the Voice that spoke; the Star which heralded the coming of the Day. St. John's self-Sacrifice showed itself in many ways. He never touched strong drink, not because it was wrong to take such things, but that he might give to the people an example of the simple life and so condemn the self-indulgeni!e that reigned round about him. By his faith- fulness to his ministry he 4howed, continuously his self-sacrifice. " Self-sacrifice!" said his Lordship. "What does our ministry cost us— whether we be clergy or laity? What does it cost us to be a priest or a deacon, or a bishop, a churchwarden or a sidesman, a day school teacher or a Sunday school teacher, a district visitor or a lay reader? What does it cost us? Does it cost us nothing? Then there is something wrong. There is nothing fruitful but self-sacrifice; and we who are followers of the Crucified Master must be ready to be self- sacrificing and self-effacing in proportion as we realise that we are men and women sent by God and entrusted by Him to do a particular work."

JOTTINGS BY AN OLD JOURNALIST.

Tee-to-turn.

The announcement of last night's open-air meeting under the auspices of the Citizens' Temperance League reminded me of certain ups and downs which have their moral. At a period in my early history, when Good Templary was booming, I was sent to report a temperance demonstration and, while watching the pro- cession I noticed in full regalia among the Good Templars a compositor at the office at which I was myself employed. Apparently in grave concern for my parlous state, he sub- sequently talked to me very seriously about the importance of signing the pledge. He was ' secretary of a Band of Hope, and he brought Ime one of their pledge cards, and—ivfell, 1 signed it; ‘and he rejoiced over me as a r brand plucked from the burning. I revisited that town a while ago, and was of course sorry as well as surprised to find that the once earnest missionary ,had had as bad a fall as Humpty Dumpty, his favourite tipple—like that of the unspeakable old Eccles in "Caste "—being rum. I considered it up to me to attempt his reclamation, and I went and had a talk with him, but I found him in adamantine chains indeed. He was about the hardest case I ever came across; he was positively wallowing in filth and gloried in his shame. He professed compassion for teetotalers, for whom,4he said, life could not really be worth living. I failed to make any impression upon this enthusiastic Good Templar of former days; in fact, he boasted /that Dr. Townsend Had actually been at pains to look him up, and had been sent away with a flea in his ear, so that (added he) I might as well save my breath to cool my porridge with. Nature Study.

The phrase used in the conclvding sentence of the foregoing caused me to turn to one of the " Cameos " in last week's issue, wherein " The Schoolmaster" gives a lesson in, or draws one from, flea-catching (of all things). ThirtY years ago I knew a young fellow who went L.) a situation in London and got into dirty "digs." "Bugs!", he exclaimed when I asked about his experiences; "Why, look here: they lurk in the staircase and set their backs up at me as I try to get to my bedroom. And the bedroom itself! Well, you may know when I tell you I've spent hours in sticking pins into the beggars and setting 'em out in a fancy pattern on the dressing table for the landlady's admiring contemplation next morning. No wonder her favourite hymn is "Lord, howdy," "Lord, howdy !" "Yes, you know—` Lord, how delightful 'tis to see.' That's what 'I like about bugs, though. Give m 9 bugs before fleas any time." "You -don't ,really mean that?" I gasped in incredulous astonishment. "I do, indeed," I was assured; "you see a bug will stop to be reasoned with, but the wicked flea, as the Psalmist terms him, is altogether too elusive for the mere male lodger—he 'ops it before one can say 'Jack Robinson: Yes, yes; I put my money on the more gentlemanly bug." I could add some personal adventures, but per- haps it isn't a very nice subject, so we'll leave it. •

Weights and Measures. The changes in the Weights and Measures Department set me reflecting on the duties of verification and inspection appertaining thereto. These are by no means light, as the law re- quires every weight measure and weighing instru- ment used in trade to be duly stamped, a uniform design of verification stamp being used by all inspectors. As a writer on the subject in the "Windsor Magazine" some years ago observed, with regard to the work of inspec- bion, it is, required that the weighing and measuring plant in use in trade ,be officially examined at least once a year, except in cases where the Board of Trade sanction an extension of this period to two years. In addition to these general visits of inspection, special surprise visits are also to be made.. As another com- mentator has noted, the reproduction of the original standards of the Imperial weights and measures deposited at the Standards Office of the Board of Trade—l know of three other centres where they have them—is an extremely delicate scientific operation. Minute variations of tempera- ture and atmospheric pressure have to be guarded against, and the fact that different substances expand in different ratios with changes of temperature, and that no substance expands uniformly, have likewise to be taken into account. THE CHURCH AND THE WAR.

Rev. T. T. James On Facing Coming Problems.

Preaching the third sermon in his course of addresses on "The Church and the War," the Rev. T. T. James, at Wycliffe Congregational Church, Warrington, on Sunday, directed the attention of his hearers to "The Church and Coming Problems." lie said that although we are loriging for peace, we are not longing for a peace which would leave unvindieated those principles of justice and honour for which we have gone to war, the peace for which we long must be founded on the attainment of those ideals of national freedom and international morality for which we have given the best our sons and offered the finest of our manhood. We look foy happier and better days when peace will be restored. We should remind ourselves as a Christian Church that the coming of that day with all the relief it will bring to us it: freedom from the horrors of war, will also usher us into a world of new and difficult prob- lems. We must by now be aware that the con- flict has Placed upon our shoulders a financial burden which has not merely to be sustained for the moment, but which will be with us as long as most of us are in the world; and we must be aware that it is a social problem the magnitude and complexity of which we have never imagined, which will be with us when the war is over. The conclusion of the war will bring with it the return of vast numbers tc our shores of those who have spent themselves withoUt stint in the cau§e of the Motherland. Granted that demobilisation must be a 'gradual process and would only be achieved with time, the fact remains that the reabsorption of these in the various spheres of industry would be a task of the first magnitude. An Unequalled Complex Situation.

Speaking of the way in which the women of this country have come forward in the hour of need, Mr. James said "It is impossible to exaggerate the value of the work done during this crisis by the women of this country, and impossible to praise too highly the way in which they have undertaken effectively tasks previously regarded as outside their capacity. Doubtless there will be many of them who will be glad enough to withdraw when opportunity arrives from duties only faced in view of a peculiar emergency, but it is equally • true that in many employments the permanence of female labour will constitute one of the factors of the situation."

Re went on to say that it was not impossible to conceive that the day would be with us in the future when there would be two men for every task instead of two tasks for every man. Beyond that we needed to remember that we shall have in our land a vast number of in- capacitated and helpless men whose service to the community has been rendered not in a life- long process, but in one supreme sacrifice by which they have lost health, strength and vigour for ever. This was the price of war, but it is a price which will have to be met, not only now when hero worship is easy, but later on when the years have passed and the romance has died away. "In all these facts,'! said Mr. James, "there' is obviously a social situation which for complexity and gravity has never been equalled. In this impending social situa- tion the Church of Jesus Christ must face its responsibility. The men and women within her ranks must realise that to these questions too much thought and consideration cannot be given." The coming days would offer a tre- mendous opportunity for the practical applica- tion of Christian thought .and Christian ideals in the realm of statesmanship and citizenship. He pleaded that all the Churches of Jesus Christ should cease at this moment to emphasise their differences, and should in a common spirit work together for the salvation of society and for the establishment of the Kingdom of God. We shall do well to realise that tho Church 'of Jesus Christ must have a social Gospel. The Gospel of Jesus Christ is not merely a message of salvation td the individual; it is a Christian programme for the common life of man. It contains principles which are to be worked out both in national life and in inter- national relationships. With all these prob- lems before us while it is our duty to view what may be the difficulty of the future, it is also our duty to be encouraged by what may be its promise.

The New Spirit of Unity.

In concluding, Mr. James said "This war, horrible as it is in many of it manifestations, has revealed many wonderful things, and amongst them nothing more wonderful than the close knitting of the hearts of men. It has welded the most widely difficult individuals into one whole by the possession of a:common 'pur- pose and a common ideal. Men have come to serve the same cause from the north and fFom the south, from the east and from the west. From the mills and the factories men have joined their brothers from the land and from the fields, and they have stood and died to- gether. The rich and the poor, the wise and the ignorant, have mingled their blood in the same trench. Heroes from the great houses of England and heroes from her lowly cottages and her back streets have gone down in the same ship and are buried in the same sea. The nation has been made one in a unity before which mere outward distinctions shrink to nothing. Shall it not be true that this spirit which can so face an outward foe shall still abide with us while we build that society which will be an expression of the Kingdom of God?"

CYCLING AND MOTORING.

WRITE TO THE MEMBER ABOUT IT.

Everyone of us is not only willing, but anxious, to sacrifice almost anything towards winning the war. Without a murmur we have sacrificed the liberty of the individual and the liberty of the Press—two of our most sacred possessions. But when it comes to the needless destruction of hard- won rights at the dictates of a policeman, it is surely time to draw the line. In several parts of the country police and magistrates alike are adopt- ing the attitude that a cyclist wheeling his bicycle is compelled to carry a light. But this is an en- tirely mistaken interpretation of the law. Here are the facts:—

According to the Local Government Act of 1888 a cyclist must be " riding or upon" his machine to commit an offence.

In the Lighting Act of 1907 handcarts are specially differentiated from cycles. In the Order of January, 11, 1916, it is stated that all vehicles and handcarts must carry lights. This is the law as it stands to-day, and I con- tend that a cyclist wheeling his machine is by law a pedestrian. He must be dealt with as such. This attempt to place the cyclist wheeling his machine in the same category as a man pushing a handcart is a direct effort on the part of some authorities to Alch away a right for which we cyclists fought hard and long. The chief difference between a cyclist and a handcart is that the cycle can be easily and quickly lifted out of the way of any ap- proaching vehicle, whereas a handcart cannot. The bicycle is the most democratic vehicle on the road, and there is neither sense nor justice in attempting to take away its rights. I would appeal to all my readers to do their bit in seeing a manifest injustice put right. One of the best ways in which they can help is to write to the local Member of Parliament about it. pointing out how unfair it is on the part of the authorities to read with the Act what is not there. THE SOUTH-EAST OF IRELAND.

To those who are in search of a quiet and peace- ful holiday, amid lovely scenery and charming People. I can strongly recommend the beautiful Blackwater district of Ireland. There is no more attractive river in all the British Isles. And yet, curious though it is, it is almost entirely neglected by the tourist, but this is rather in its favour if one wants a rest. I know of no better way of spending one's vacation than awheel a good bicycle in this lovely valley. Starting from and finishing at Fermoy. on the main line between Cork and Waterford and Rosslare, make for Cappoquin, Vil- liorstown and Youghal—a round of only 67 miles, it's true, but every inch of which fills one with delight. For the first few miles after leaving Fer- moy there is nothing particular to note, but as one approaches Cappoquin the scenery becomes remark- ably fine, while the village tself is a charming spot amidst glorious surroundings. The fishing here, too, is extremely good. The place is of great antiquity, and had anciently a castle erected by the Fitzgerald family. In the war of 1641-45 it was the scene of several battles. At Lismore, near by, the Duke of Devonshire owns a stately castle, which can be seen for many miles around. From Cappoquin the southern road is taken, and from thence to Youghal one rides along the east side of the river all the way. Some of the views are very fine indeed, and the road is an easy one. Youghal, an ancient and pretty town, is partly a holiday resort and partly a port with a small coast- ing and fishing trade. Sir Walter Raleigh was chief magistrate here in 1588, and his house is still standing, in the garden of which he had his •first smoke and planted the first potato in Ireland.

Women In Industry. The war has removed many barriers to the employment of women in industries and occu- pations, and these women find in their work a zest and an independence that they will be most unwilling to relinquish when peace comes again. Not only are these workers benefiting themselves, but their services are of the gut- most value to the _community at large, and that they will be called upon to resign their positions is highly improbable. The war has set up numerous new conditions in the life of the country, and the colossal work of repairing the devastation caused by the war in Europe will entail an enormous drain on the inter- national labour markets for years to come. What has been destroyed will have to be re- stored, and in this work women's labour will meet one of the needs of the future as of the present. A Practical Exhibition.

Among many novel features at the National Economy Exhibition at Prince's Skating Club is the converting of old gowns into new. A party of ladies advise on the transformation of old-fashioned gowns and hats into the latest modes. A clear and clever drawing supple- ments the advice, and enables even an amateur to follow the directions easily. The, care of clothes is also important from an economical point of view, and a party of French girls give demonstrations on the right way to fold dresses, brush hats, and keep laces and frills at their best; also how to make and to trim hats smartly, but very cheaply. In the kitchen section experts ticlvise as to the best centres for buying commodities, and information is also ' given on shopping in the markets. Through what hands and scenes they have since passed none can tell, but assuredly the most wonderful chapter in their story is, that after all these years, numbers of them are being picked up in 1916 on the battlefields of Europe and are finding their way back to London as souvenirs.

WARRINGTON-GET THAT

TRADE!

There are three ways in which we can show our resentment of the conduct of Germany and Austria, and their dupes, the Turks and Bulgarians; and the Warrington Chamber of Commerce has adopted one a the best two of those three ways. The methods to which we refer are the erection of high tariff barriers against commercial intercourse hereafter with our present enemies; the education of our people to cope with and defeat those enemies in fair and equal competition; and the organisation of our trading interests so that we shall not be outclassed in the future, as in the past,- by our wily adversaries. There is a loud cry, for the introduction of tariffs as against the Central Powers, and partisans go so far as to say that Tariff Reform has come to pass• as a result of the war. Has it? Assuming that the Central Powers are " pun- ished " for their misdeeds by beink ostracised by international traders, that would not be Tariff Reform. It might even lead to an ex- tension of Free Trade. If the Allies, strong' in finance as they are overwhelming in man force and material resources, send the Central Powers to Coventry, they might conversel3- set un a -species of Free Trade among them- selves. That would ,not be the triumph of Free' Trade, any more than the other side of the picture would be the triumph of Tariff Reform. But we sincerely hope politics And party shibboleths will be left out of con- sideration when this question, so important to our perpetual welfare, is being settled. In any case, it will be distinctly understood, if we Undertake to "punish Germany, that we know eieaetly what we are doing, and that we ire acting with our eyes open. If we are anxious for revenge we shall be .willing, of course, to pay the price; and if we damage ourselveq as well as our foes we must accept our bruises calmly and philosophically. We incline rather to the side of those who would set their house in order and so beat the Huns by sheer merit, as there is no doubt we could do. As we have previously pointed out, the British brain is superior, in almost all branches of scientific work, to the Ger- man brain, but our opponents have the superior sense to apply the results that we have placed at their disposal. A homely illus- tration of what we mean is seen in our treat- ment of the daylight saving suggestion. We toyed with our idea for years, the Germans applied it, and then we, rather ignominiously, copied them in the application of our own scheme. We want properly trained men and women for carrying on the life and trade of the country after the war; but when in one year we have nearly two hundred boys and girls who are anxious to pursue their studies in order to fit themselves, by higher educa- tion, for undertaking that work in the days to come there is accommodation at our Second- ary School for only sixty of them. The others are unceremoniously " scrapped ; and the pity is that Warrington is not isolated in this matter. War " economy " has spread to our education. A nation that economises on edu- cation saves'. as a man would save who de- clined to feed. himself. Let us get ourselves educated so that efficiency may win through, rather than trust to being propped up artifi- cially.. That brings us to the question of organisa- tion. We rejoice to think that the Warring- ton Chamber of Commerce, in conjunction with many other similar bodies, have already taken decisive steps. They intend to issue a Register, in five languages, of manufacturers in this district who export, or wish to become exporters. Ten thousand copies of the book are to be printed and placed gratuitously at the disposal of consuls, commercial attaches, consular agents boards of trades, commercial institutions and the principal buyers through- out the Dominions and Allied and neutral countries. There is a gigantic mass of trade in all parts of the world that can be had by us for the asking. Some of it belonged to Germany; more of it will belong to Germany unless we move, and move quickly. The Ger- man export trade to Russia alone was worth £40,000,000 a year. Russia expects us to, go and pick ,up that trade and the developments upon that trade which are bound to follow. Will we oblige our Allies by making them • really independent of Germany by sending our young men there to learn their language, and welcoming their young men here to learn ours? Will, we do the like by France, by Be!-

KR. HAROLD SMITH, M.P., AND HOME RULE. Ireland's Right To Govern Herself Conceded, Temporarily. THE LESSER RISK.

To the Editor of "The Warrington Examiner." Sir,—l shall be glad if you can publish llie enclosed in your next issue.—Yours, &e., TIAROLD KUM.

Sir,—On the eve of the meeting of Unionist Members Of Parliament, to be held at the Carlton Club, I ask your leave to make a short preliminary statement as to the decision I have arrived at after very careful and anxious thought with reference to the pro- posal to arrange a temporary settlement of the Irish Question until the end of the war. I hope- that I have satisfied my constituents that I have never entertained one thought of party politics since the outbreak of the war, and in pursuance of this policy I ask myself one question—one question only—What will best conduce to the more efficient prosecution of the war?

After the most earnest consideration I have decided that under the present circumstances less demands will be made upon our military resources if we concede to Ireland, temporarily, the right to govern herself, of course excluding the parts of Ulster as agreed between Sir Edward Carson and Mr. Redmond. I am under no sort of illusion as to the atti- tude of a certain, and, I believe, a large section of the Irish people. I am satisfied that a large force will still have to be kept in Ireland, and that the power to enforce the Defence of the Realm Acts must be retained by the Imperial Government. I appreciate that we are- compelled to accept a risk what- ever decision is arrived at, but I am now con- vinced that we run the lesser risk of further rebellion during the war if we seek the co- operation of the best elements in Ireland and leave in their hands the, task of rallying to the Crown classes hitherto hostile. This is, I repeat, a risk, a great' risk; in ,my view it would be a greater risk to face during the war the possibility of continued rebellion, leaping out from time to time in various parts and involving large numbers of troops safe- guarding life and property and necessarily shooting down these misguided and rebellious spirits. I owe it to my constituents generally to give my reasons more fully than I can by means of a letter, and I ow:e it particularly to my Cen- tral Council to give in detail my reasons for arriving at a decision which will at first possibly cause many of them some surprise. I shall look forward to the discharge Of both their obligations at an early date, and I hope that I shall satisfy the preponderating majority of all parties .that the proposals I intend to accept will enable us to present a united front to the enemy which is seeking to devastate Europe. —I am, sir, yours, tte., HAROLD SMITH. House of Commons, July 6th.

AFTER-WAR PROBLEMS.

Capt. Mathams Urges Prepara- tion For Peace Conditions.

Captain Mathams visited the Wycliffe P.S.A. on Sunday, and deliverecitan interesting address on after-war problems.

Industries,' he said, had been disorganised and would liave to be carefully dealt with. The great danger after the war would be the flood- ing of the labour market, and if demobilisation was not carried out scientifically by the mili- tary and civil authorities the affairs would not go smoothly either for soldiers or industry. He suggested that the fact that our Army was Territorial in essence made possible the de- mobilisation ok battalions according to the character of the men composing them, in such a manner as to meet the immediate require- ments of various industries. For instance, he illustrated, if the cotton industry "picked up" quickly it would be very easy for Oldham bat- talions to be demobilised, but if the demand for labour in this industry was not very great the difficulty of doing all that was best for the men would be great. There was a possibility that if the supply of labour at the finish of the war was in excess of the demand the resulting drop in wages without a corresponding drop in prices would cause a great deal of social unrest, and he doubted Whether this country was suffi- ciently educated in the mass to prevent the possibility of their seeking in another war relief from the financial strain in which they might find themselves. It was also possible that as many women had been put in positions of economic independ- ence, and those who had stayed at home had enjoyed high wages, they might be very much dissatisfied, and be liable to seek prosperity in another war, though, of course, ,money was only one factor; the love of human life might dis- suade them from entering into other quarrels. But still, continued prosperity for all the people was to be aimed at. Masters and men would have to help in putting things into a satisfac- tory position., We had entered into this war in the name of righteousness and truth unitedly, so we would have to be united in making things best for all. Our duties to one another must ever be before us. Men had sacrificed their lives for a great object, and we shou4l meet all the difficulties in the same spirit. The Germans had required forty years to prepare for war, and as peace was a greater thing than war it was the duty of citizenship to prepare for peace. The Rev. T. T. James presided over the meet- ing, and welcomed several soldiers who were home on leave. Mr. Poole, of the Parish Church Choir, sang in an able manner "Coming nf a dream" and "Thou art passing hence." Mr. W. H. Payton presided at the organ. A most interesting meeting was closed by the singing of "Rouse then, ye soldiers."

WITH THE WOUNDED.

Nurses' Smart Victory.

Cricket has suddenly become the principal sport in Lymm, and the wounded soldiers from Brookfield have not been slow to take up the opportunities put before them by their sports organisers. Several cricket matches have taken place during the past week, and judging by the fair number of spectators that assembled on the cricket ground last Wednesday after- noon, the public of Lymm still take a deep interest in this particular branch of sport. Lymm at one time prided itself on its smart cricket-team, but at the present time most mem- bers of this team are batting away with in- creased energy against the terrific onslaughts of the Huns, and it is left to those at home to keep the old sport alive. Jones' Team v. Knight's Team.

The first match on Wednesday was between teams picked by two wounded warriors at the hospital, and included the nurses. Quite half the teams were members of the fairer sex. The match itself was full of thrills, and interest- ing moments, for to see " Tommy " being coached to run a little faster by his feminine partner was an absolutely new experience, 'and created plenty of good humour. In the end Jones' team ran out easy winners by 56 runs to 19 runs.

" Tomm les " v.- Nurses.

After a short vacation, specially reserved for the wants of ' the inner man, had been taken, the principal match of the day took place, namely, the " Tommies " v. Nurses match. The teams were chosen on the ground, and were captain by the irresistible Jones and Miss May Saville respectively. One of the conditions set down in the match was that " Tommy " should bat left-handed. One of the chief disadvantages of the nurses' team was that they could only command one bowler. This was the captain, and she had to take both ends and stick it throughout the innings. Nevertheless, the work' of destruction went on, and in the end " Tommies' " team were forced to retire with a score of 25 runs to their credit. The nurses took their innings in grand style and, confident of victory, they kept on increasing their score, and when the innings came to an end it was found that they. had 32 runs to their credit, which made them victors by seven clear runs. If the match was not fast and furious, it cer- tainly was slow and humorous.

The match on Saturday between Warrington and the Brookfield team, as was expected. turned out an easy victory for Warrington by 103 for seven wickets, to 69 all out.

THE EXAMINER, SATURDAY, JULY 8, 1916.

(:.LL FOR VOLUNTEERS.

To the Editor of "The Examiner."

Sir,--1 wish to make an appeal through your columns to the men of Lymm and the sur- rounding district to join the Cheshire Volun- teer Regiment. The Volunteer movement has now obtained the full recognition of the mili- tary authorities and the services of many of the county regiments (including the Cheshire Regiment) have been accepted by His Majesty the King, under the Volunteer Act of 1863. _ This, Act provides that, in case' of imminent threatened invasion, those enrolled may be called upon to repel the enemy at any part of the United Kingdom. Any other duties, such as guards, patrols, &c., would be purely volun- tary, and times and places would be chosen that would not interfere with the ordinary avocations of those who volunteer specially for these extra duties. Anyone wishing to retire from the corps can do so by giving fourteen days', notice, in writing, to his commanding officer. Of course, this would not apply if on active service.

Many Tribunals are now making it a condi- tion of exemption that the appellant should join a Volunteer regiment. This has not been done so far in Lymin, but that the Tribunal has that power has ;not been lost sight of by the members. I would appeal most earnestly to every man in Lymm and district who has obtained absolute, conditional, or temporary exemption (if for a lengthy period) to enrol at Once, thereby giving himself the right to take up arms to defend his country, his home, and himself, were such a disaster as- an _invasion of this country to become a reality. Drills and musketry practices are held at the Drill Hall, Lymm, every Tuesday and Thursday evenings at 8 p.m. If t6n or more new recruits join the Lymm Platoon I shall be quite willing to form a recruits' class on Wed- nesday evening at 8 o'clock, until such time as they are far enough advanced to take their places with those who have been drilling for a longer period. Enrolments can be made any Tuesday or Thursday at the Drill Hall. I cannot do better than end this appeal by a quotation from, a letter of Major Alan Sykes (the champion of the Defence Movement in the House of Commons) to "The Manchester Guar- dian," dated June 2nd, 1916, as follows "May I therefore ask every Volunteer, and everybody who is interested in the Volunteer movement, and everybody who has at heart the defence of the realm against invasion to bear in mind that our Volunteers are now an intrinsic part of the defence of the realm, and that any assistance given to, them, either by personal service or laS, any other means, is part of our national organised gcheme of mobilising the whole forces of the nation, by which mobil- isation alone we can hope to defeat the organ- ised efforts of our adversaries ?" -

Yours, &c., G. R. WILLIAMSON, Platoon Commander, No. 1 Platoon, E Co., Altrincham Batt., C.V.R.

THE CLUB ON THE CAUSEWAY.

Far from the madding crowd, but con- veniently situated near the busy town, the ground of the Causeway Lawn Tennis Club in Causeway-avenue, is very popular amongst its members.

The club has quite an interesting history, as it has been in existence for over a quarter of a century. It was originally connected with the old Y.M.C.A. down Arpley way, but has had its comfortable quarters at the end of 'Cause- way-avenue for the past six years or so. Here is a nicely secluded spot, there are five tennis 'courts and a croquet lawn, the sward being neatly trimmed and showing evidence of most -careful attention. A tea room and dressing rooms for members have been ingeniously pro- vided, and many enjoyable afternoons and evenings have been spent by our club folk and their friends. The club is pre-eminently a social one, and although competitions and challenge matches have taken place from time to time, pleasant intercourse and friendly rivalry are the outstanding features of this happy. association. Inter-club and other matches have been abandoned two years. Naturally, owing to the'war the membership of the club has been somewhat depleted, and amongst those who have joined, the Colours are Messrs. Welsby, Sutton, Mercer, Travis and Welding, while Miss Bird and miss Wyatt are honoured lady members who have taken over Red Cross work.

In common with other clubs, the Causeway Lawn Tennis Club adores the Daylight Saving Act: It is marvellous what an amount of extra enjoyment that ingenious pushing on of the hands of the clock for an hour has provided. It frequently happened that players who got home fairly late from town had little chame of getting a game, all the courts being in full swing when they arrived at the ground. Now the lengthened days mean better opportunity for play all round. And so every evening the ground is well patronised. But Saturday afternoon is an auspicious time. Then tea is provided by the lady mem- bers—who take it in turn to cater for the club— and everyone is gay and exhilarated and springy and anxious to hear again that extra- ordinary remark by the scorer "Love all." Last Saturday week was a special day, when in pleasant weather some convalescent soldiers— including two non-corns--from Raddon Court Red Cross Hospital, were present on the in- vitation, of the club. They were hospitably entertained, and had a high old time. It was kind and thoughtful of the committee, and the genial hon. treasurer, Mr. Ernest Hawkesworth, was generous enough to inform this deponent that "other clubs" were doing like acts of kindness. Several photographs of the company were taken during the afternoon. At the head of this well-ordered club is Lady Greenall, who is its honoured preAdent, and here it should be mentioned that the ladies con- tribute largely to its success—they pay up "like men" and throw in the charm of their presence gratis. By a little care and forethought it is possible to reduce your tyre bill by 25 per cent. When the car is in the garage, and it is not going to be used during the next 24 hours, it pays to jack UP all four wheels. This relieves the tyres consider- ably. Last year I got our local wheelwright to cut me three (the jack takes the place of the fourth one) blocks of wood, four inches in diameter, and suffi- ciently long to raise the wheels about a couple of inches off the ground when placed beneath the a,xle- They cost Ss. 4d.. and have saved me pounds. Loose road metal plays havoc with tyres, especially if the car is driven quickly' over it. Never put on the brakes when passing over rough metal. Pass over the part, if you can, with the clutch out; if yoU cannot do this then on low gear. Never accelerate when going over unrolled metal. Where possible, keep two of the wheels off the metal, if this can be managed without running over scattered stones' If not, then take the car right over the centre of the metalled road.
